# Regulatory references (summaries for retrieval)

Summaries of the public regulator documents listed in the dataset README. They are paraphrased for grounding; the source documents are authoritative.

## FinCEN SAR narrative guidance: the five Ws and how
A SAR narrative must stand on its own. It should answer: **who** is conducting the suspicious activity (customers, account and card identifiers, devices, counterparties); **what** instruments or mechanisms were used (card-not-present purchases, card-present purchases, amounts); **when** the activity occurred (first and last dates, time pattern); **where** it took place (channel, billing region, device, online merchant); **why** it is suspicious (deviation from the customer's normal activity, red flags, links to other fraud); and **how** it was carried out (method of operation such as testing a stolen number, account takeover, structuring below a threshold). Write in chronological order, state facts plainly, avoid unexplained codes and do not include supporting documents in the narrative itself.

## FinCEN: preparing a complete and sufficient SAR narrative
Common deficiencies are blank or boilerplate narratives, missing amounts or dates, and failing to explain why the activity is suspicious. Include the total dollar amount, the relationship between subjects, and the investigation steps that led to filing. Describe what action the institution took (card blocked, account monitored).

## FinCEN SAR supporting documentation (FIN-2007-G003)
Institutions must keep the supporting documentation for five years from filing and make it available to law enforcement on request. The internal case file (evidence, transactions, analyst decisions) is that documentation, which is why a SAR always has a case behind it.

## FinCEN advisory on account takeover (FIN-2011-A016)
Account takeover is when a criminal uses stolen credentials to control a customer's account. Red flags include: logins or purchases from new devices or unusual IP addresses and proxies, changes to contact details such as email domains, activity across channels that does not fit the customer, and sudden bursts of transactions. Institutions should report account takeover as a SAR when thresholds are met and describe the access method.

## FinCEN: identity-related suspicious activity (2021)
Identity-related abuse shows up as impersonation, compromised credentials and misuse of devices and identity attributes shared across many unrelated customers. Shared device fingerprints, email domains or addresses across unrelated accounts in a short window are indicators of organised activity and should be named in the SAR.

## FinCEN advisory on imposter scams and money mules
Coordinated fraud rings route stolen value through many accounts. Indicators: the same device, IP or recipient identity used by many unrelated customers; repeated similar amounts; rapid succession. Name every linked account in the report.

## FATF: illicit financial flows from cyber-enabled fraud
Cyber-enabled fraud is typically organised and transnational and uses stolen card data, account takeover and mule networks. FATF recommends that institutions detect networked activity (shared infrastructure across victims) and file reports that link cases instead of treating them as isolated incidents.

## FFIEC BSA/AML manual: SAR requirements and red flags
Banks must file a SAR for known or suspected violations where the aggregate amount meets thresholds, generally within 30 days of initial detection. Red flags relevant to cards: transactions structured just below reporting or authorization thresholds; unusual velocity; transactions inconsistent with the customer's profile; many accounts sharing common identifiers.
